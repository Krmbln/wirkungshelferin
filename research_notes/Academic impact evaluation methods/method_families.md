# Academic Impact Evaluation Method Families & Evaluation-Community Hubs

Scope: methodological, context-independent sources that a small nonprofit (single non-expert user, ~1 h/week) could use, and that we as designers of an AI assistant (7-step method guide: 0 context, 1 target groups, 2 problem & root causes, 3 ToC incl. assumptions, 4 indicators, 5 data collection methods, 6 data collection plan) can use to write method content and quality rubrics.

Baseline catalogue already covered (not repeated): PHINEO Kursbuch Wirkung, Aspen ToC facilitator guide, PHINEO Wirkungsrad, IRIS+/IMP five dimensions + IMP self-reported data, SRS 2014, Social Value International Standards 2025 draft, Matatihi SROI guide.

Verification note: URLs below were confirmed via search results or fetches in this session unless marked "(URL not verified)". Some descriptive details are from the researcher's background knowledge; these are flagged "(background knowledge, not re-verified)".

## Q1. BetterEvaluation (Rainbow Framework) — what it is, canonical pages, relevance

### Takeaway
BetterEvaluation is the single best free "map" of evaluation tasks and options; its Rainbow Framework's DEFINE cluster (develop initial description, develop programme theory/ToC, identify unintended results) maps almost 1:1 onto our steps 0–3, and DESCRIBE maps onto steps 4–6. Too broad for the end user to navigate alone; very high value for us as designers (as a checklist for completeness and as a source of option-lists).

### Cited Findings
- The Rainbow Framework organises evaluation tasks into seven clusters: manage; define what is to be evaluated; frame the boundaries; describe activities, outcomes, impact and context; understand causes; synthesise; report and support use — [BetterEvaluation, Rainbow Framework project](https://betterevaluation.org/node/2775); [AEA/BetterEvaluation webinar series](https://comm.eval.org/coffee_break_webinars/coffeebreak/betterevalseries)
- DEFINE tasks = developing an initial description, developing or refining the programme theory/theory of change, and identifying possible unintended results — [BetterEvaluation "52 weeks: Defining what needs to be evaluated"](https://betterevaluation.org/node/2418); [ODI webinar "Define what is to be evaluated"](https://odi.org/en/events/betterevaluation-webinar-define-what-is-to-be-evaluated/)
- Downloadable Rainbow Framework PDF generator exists on the site — [betterevaluation.org/generate/framework/2646/pdf](https://betterevaluation.org/generate/framework/2646/pdf)
- BetterEvaluation became part of the Global Evaluation Initiative (GEI) in 2022 (announced 29 Nov 2022) and "continues to be free and open access" — [BetterEvaluation news: Becomes Part of GEI](https://betterevaluation.org/node/2940); [GEI's BetterEvaluation launches revamped platform](https://glocalevalweek.org/node/62243)
- The site hosts dedicated method pages that also serve as indexes to the other sources below, e.g. Contribution Analysis — [BetterEvaluation: Contribution analysis](https://betterevaluation.org/node/2708); Outcome Harvesting — [BetterEvaluation resource](https://betterevaluation.org/node/1580); Rubric Revolution — [BetterEvaluation: The rubric revolution](https://www.betterevaluation.org/tools-resources/rubric-revolution)
- Origins (background knowledge, not re-verified): founded ~2012 by Patricia Rogers (RMIT, later ANZSOG) with ODI, Pact, ILAC and others; Rogers was CEO; Monash/ANZSOG hosted it before transfer to GEI.

### Inferences
- Steps informed: 0 (Define → initial description), 2–3 (develop programme theory; identify unintended results), 4 (Describe → "specify the key indicators"), 5 (Describe → collect/retrieve data options list: individuals, groups, observation, existing records), 6 (Manage → "develop evaluation plan or framework"; "determine and secure resources").
- Beyond our catalogue: (a) the explicit task "identify possible UNINTENDED results" (incl. negative) — PHINEO/Aspen do not force this; strong candidate for a rubric criterion at step 3; (b) "Frame" cluster: who are the primary intended users and what are the intended uses — a proportionality question we should ask at step 0; (c) a broad, neutral option catalogue for data collection (step 5) beyond PHINEO's method overview.
- Verdict user: medium-low (too many options; risk of overwhelm). Verdict designers: HIGH — use the DEFINE/FRAME/DESCRIBE task lists as a completeness checklist for our guide.

### Gaps
- Exact current URLs for the individual "Develop programme theory/ToC" and "Specify the key indicators" pages after the 2022 platform relaunch were not verified (old /en/rainbow_framework/... paths were restructured). Licence of site content (CC BY-NC-SA historically) not re-verified.
- No BetterEvaluation page specifically targeted at "small organisations" was found in this session.

## Q2. Contribution Analysis (John Mayne)

### Takeaway
Contribution Analysis (CA) is the most directly useful academic method for our use case: it legitimises "contribution, not attribution" for small projects without control groups and turns the ToC + assumptions + rival explanations into a "contribution story" that is checked against evidence. A lightweight version (ToC with assumptions + "what else could explain this?") is suitable for the user; the full six steps are for designers.

### Cited Findings
- Canonical: Mayne, J. (2008) "Contribution analysis: An approach to exploring cause and effect", ILAC Brief 16 — [RePEc/IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ags/ilacbr/52525.html); [CGSpace record mirror](https://buscador.una.edu.ni/Record/CGSpace70124?lng=en); [onthinktanks summary](https://onthinktanks.org/articles/contribution-analysis-by-john-mayne/)
- CA "can provide credible assessments of cause and effect when it is not practical to design an experiment"; it explores attribution by assessing the contribution a programme makes, verifying the theory of change while taking into account other influencing factors — [BetterEvaluation: Contribution analysis](https://betterevaluation.org/node/2708); [onthinktanks](https://onthinktanks.org/articles/contribution-analysis-by-john-mayne/)
- Steps (as summarised): set out the cause-effect issue; develop the theory of change and risks to it, including rival explanations; gather existing evidence; assemble and assess the contribution story; seek additional evidence; revise and strengthen the contribution story — [onthinktanks](https://onthinktanks.org/articles/contribution-analysis-by-john-mayne/); [whatworks.org.nz](https://whatworks.org.nz/contribution-analysis/)
- Update: Mayne, J. (2019) "Revisiting Contribution Analysis", Canadian Journal of Program Evaluation 34(2): 171–191, journal is open-access — [CJPE article page](https://ces.journals.uvic.ca/index.php/cjpe/article/view/284)
- A 2024 CJPE special issue commemorates Mayne, incl. "John Mayne and Rules of Thumb for Contribution Analysis" — [CJPE](https://ces.journals.uvic.ca/index.php/cjpe/article/view/147); [Mapping the Contributions of John Mayne](https://ces.journals.uvic.ca/index.php/cjpe/article/view/141)
- Practitioner add-on combining CA with Bayesian updating — [Itad briefing paper, April 2018](https://itad.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/CDC-Brief-contribution-analysis-and-Bayesian-Updating.pdf)
- Background knowledge (not re-verified in this session): Mayne's 2019 paper introduces the "COM-B" based ToC model (capability, opportunity, motivation → behaviour change) and distinguishes "causal link assumptions"; he also frames the "plausibility" of a contribution claim as depending on whether assumptions are supported by evidence and rival explanations are addressed.

### Inferences
- Steps informed: 3 (ToC + assumptions + explicit "other influencing factors/rival explanations"), 4 (indicators placed on the links where assumptions are weakest), 5–6 (seek evidence where the contribution story is weakest = prioritisation rule for data collection).
- Beyond catalogue: PHINEO and Aspen name assumptions but do not ask "what else could have caused this?" (rival explanations / external factors). SROI's attribution/deadweight addresses this numerically (Matatihi); CA addresses it narratively and is far more feasible for a non-expert. Suggested rubric criterion for step 3: "names at least 1–2 other factors that could also explain the hoped-for change."
- Also gives a principled answer to "we have no control group" → aim for a "credible contribution story", which aligns with SVI's "do not overclaim".
- Verdict user: HIGH in light form (2–3 guided questions). Verdict designers: HIGH (wording of claims, prioritising data collection, a "contribution story" output at step 6).

### Gaps
- Direct free PDF of ILAC Brief 16 was not opened in this session (ILAC site URLs have changed; CGSpace hosts it). Exact text of the six steps should be checked against the original brief before quoting.

## Q3. Outcome Harvesting (Wilson-Grau & Britt, Ford Foundation 2012)

### Takeaway
Outcome Harvesting works backwards from observed changes (incl. unplanned ones) to how the project contributed. It is a strong complement for capturing unexpected outcomes but is designed for evaluators/complex advocacy work; for the user only a micro-version ("log observed changes in behaviour of actors + your contribution") is realistic.

### Cited Findings
- Canonical: Wilson-Grau, R. & Britt, H. (2012, revised Nov 2013) "Outcome Harvesting", Ford Foundation brief, 27 pages — [PDF (May 2012) via USAID Learning Lab](https://www.usaidlearninglab.org/system/files/resource/files/Outome%20Harvesting%20Brief%20FINAL%202012-05-2-1_0.pdf); [Revised Nov 2013 PDF](https://cnxus.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/wilsongrau_en_Outome20Harvesting20Brief_revised20Nov202013.pdf)
- It is "a utilisation-focused, highly participatory tool" to "identify, formulate, verify, and make sense of outcomes" when cause–effect relations are unknown; it "does not measure progress towards predetermined outcomes" but collects evidence of what has been achieved and works backward to determine whether and how the intervention contributed — [summary of the brief via search; BetterEvaluation](https://betterevaluation.org/node/1580); [BEAM Exchange](https://beamexchange.org/resources/tools/610/)
- Further resources hub — [outcomeharvesting.net General OH Resources](https://www.outcomeharvesting.net/resources/general)
- Background knowledge (not re-verified): an "outcome" in OH = an observable change in behaviour, relationships, actions, policies or practices of a social actor influenced by the intervention; six steps: design the harvest, gather data/draft outcome descriptions, engage informants, substantiate, analyse & interpret, support use. Wilson-Grau's 2019 book (IAP) is not free.

### Inferences
- Steps informed: 4 (qualitative "outcome description" as an indicator alternative), 5 (method option: harvesting from reports/staff/participants), 3 (feeding back unexpected outcomes into the ToC).
- Beyond catalogue: systematic capture of UNPLANNED outcomes + substantiation by a third party; our catalogue is mostly forward-planned (Wirkungstreppe, SMART). Simplest transferable element: an "outcome log" template (who changed, what, when, how did we contribute, who can confirm).
- Verdict user: LOW–MEDIUM (full method too demanding; micro "outcome log" feasible). Verdict designers: MEDIUM (template idea; criteria for a well-formed outcome statement).

### Gaps
- Licence of the Ford brief not confirmed (freely distributed but licence terms not checked).

## Q4. Realist Evaluation (Pawson & Tilley; RAMESES)

### Takeaway
Realist evaluation asks "what works, for whom, in what circumstances, and why" via context–mechanism–outcome (CMO) configurations. Too demanding for the user as a method; the CMO question is, however, an excellent prompt for steps 1 and 3 ("for which subgroup / under which conditions does this work, and why?").

### Cited Findings
- Realist evaluation is "a form of theory-driven evaluation, based on realist philosophy that aims to understand why these complex interventions work, how, for whom, in what context and to what extent" — [RAMESES II protocol, BMJ Open 2015 (open access)](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/5/8/e008567); [PMC version](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4538260)
- RAMESES II produced reporting standards for realist evaluations plus training materials, freely available at www.ramesesproject.org — [RAMESES II protocol](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4538260/); [Oxford RAMESES projects page](https://www.phc.ox.ac.uk/research/interdisciplinary-research-in-health-sciences/rameses)
- Reporting standards: Wong et al. (2016) "RAMESES II reporting standards for realist evaluations", BMC Medicine — [open-access PDF](https://bmcmedicine.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12916-016-0643-1)
- Full NIHR report (Health Services and Delivery Research, Oct 2017) — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/c1abde0272d84a798da6ae922aec0a8b)
- Background knowledge (not re-verified): Pawson & Tilley's "Realistic Evaluation" (1997, Sage) is a paywalled book; the RAMESES site hosts free quality standards, "theory/mechanism" explainers and FAQs.

### Inferences
- Steps informed: 1 (differentiate target subgroups — "for whom"), 3 (mechanism = the "because" in an assumption; context = conditions), 5 (interviews asking "why did this work for you?").
- Beyond catalogue: the explicit concept of MECHANISM (the reasoning/response of participants triggered by resources) is missing from PHINEO/Aspen, which speak of preconditions and assumptions. A rubric criterion for step 3: "each key arrow has a 'because…' (why would people react this way?)". Also: "for whom does it NOT work?" as an equity/heterogeneity prompt.
- Verdict user: LOW as a method (expert method, requires theory building and qualitative analysis skills); prompt-level borrowing is HIGH value. Verdict designers: MEDIUM–HIGH.

### Gaps
- Did not open ramesesproject.org directly to confirm the current location of training materials and the standards' licence.

## Q5. Outcome Mapping (IDRC) / ODI RAPID ROMA / Outcome Mapping Learning Community

### Takeaway
Outcome Mapping shifts focus to changes in behaviour, relationships and actions of "boundary partners" the project directly works with, with graded "progress markers" (expect to see / like to see / love to see). The progress-marker ladder is a very usable indicator device for small projects; the full OM method is too heavy. ROMA is policy-influence specific and largely irrelevant.

### Cited Findings
- Canonical: Earl, S., Carden, F. & Smutylo, T. (2001) "Outcome Mapping: Building Learning and Reflection into Development Programs", IDRC — [PDF (theoryofchange.org library)](https://www.theoryofchange.org/wp-content/uploads/toco_library/pdf/2001-Earl-Book-Outcome-mapping-building-learning.pdf); [PDF (Africa Portal)](https://africaportal.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Outcome_Mapping_Building_Learning_and_Reflection_into_Development_Programs.pdf)
- OM "shifts away from assessing the products of a program to focus on changes in behaviour, relationships, actions, and activities in the people, groups, and organizations it works with directly" — [search summary of IDRC book; docs.opendeved.net record](https://docs.opendeved.net/lib/HHNIBZF6)
- ROMA: Young, J., Shaxson, L., Jones, H., Hearn, S., Datta, A. & Cassidy, C. (2014) "ROMA: A guide to policy engagement and influence", ODI — a suite of tools to diagnose the problem, set realistic objectives for policy influence, plan, monitor and learn — [ODI page](https://odi.org/en/about/features/roma-a-guide-to-policy-engagement-and-policy-influence/); [PDF via ANU i2s](https://i2s.anu.edu.au/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/9011.pdf)
- ROMA includes stakeholder tools like the Alignment-Interest-Influence Matrix (AIIM) — [ODI publication](https://www.odi.org/publications/5288-stakeholder-engagement-stakeholder-analysis-aiim-alignment-interest-influence-matrix-roma)
- Background knowledge (not re-verified): progress markers graded "expect to see / like to see / love to see"; "sphere of control / influence / interest"; the Outcome Mapping Learning Community (outcomemapping.ca) was a free community and resource library, its status after ~2020 not verified.

### Inferences
- Steps informed: 1 (boundary partners = whose behaviour do we directly influence), 3 (sphere of control/influence/interest — sets realistic outcome boundaries, matches "do not overclaim"), 4 (progress markers as graded qualitative indicators), 5 (outcome journals).
- Beyond catalogue: graded progress-marker ladder is a gentler alternative to SMART targets for behaviour change; "sphere of influence" is a clean rule for where the project's accountability ends (PHINEO's Wirkungstreppe levels 4–7 partially cover this).
- Verdict user: MEDIUM for progress markers + spheres; LOW for full OM. ROMA: NOT relevant (policy influencing). Designers: MEDIUM.

### Gaps
- Current status/URL of the Outcome Mapping Learning Community not verified.

## Q6. 3ie — Theory-based impact evaluation (Howard White)

### Takeaway
White's six principles of theory-based impact evaluation are a concise statement of what a good causal chain should contain (map the chain, context, heterogeneity, counterfactual, factual analysis, mixed methods). Useful as a designer reference; the counterfactual requirement is out of reach for most small projects and must be explicitly relaxed for our users.

### Cited Findings
- Canonical: White, H. (2009) "Theory-Based Impact Evaluation: Principles and Practice", 3ie Working Paper 3; also in Journal of Development Effectiveness 1(3): 271–284 — [IDEAS working paper](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ris/iiierp/2009_003.html); [JDE record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/jdevef/v1y2009i3p271-284.html); [GSDRC summary](https://gsdrc.org/document-library/theory-based-impact-evaluation-principles-and-practice/)
- Six principles: (1) map out the causal chain (programme theory); (2) understand context; (3) anticipate heterogeneity; (4) rigorous evaluation of impact using a credible counterfactual; (5) rigorous factual analysis; (6) use mixed methods — [GSDRC](https://gsdrc.org/document-library/theory-based-impact-evaluation-principles-and-practice/); [ALNAP](https://alnap.org/help-library/resources/theory-based-impact-evaluation/)
- 3ie principles for impact evaluation (PDF) — [ALNAP library](https://library.alnap.org/system/files/content/resource/files/main/3ieprinciplesforimpactevaluation.pdf)
- White on small-n impact evaluation — [World Bank Development Impact blog guest post](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/impactevaluations/guest-post-by-howard-white-can-we-do-small-n-impact-evaluations)

### Inferences
- Steps informed: 3 (causal chain + context + heterogeneity), 4 (indicators along the whole chain, not just final outcomes — "rigorous factual analysis": did the inputs/outputs actually happen, did the target group take part?), 5 (mixed methods).
- Beyond catalogue: "factual analysis" — checking each link of the chain empirically (reach, participation, quality) — is a cheap, high-value practice for small projects; it reinforces PHINEO's lower Wirkungstreppe levels as a legitimate evidence base.
- Verdict user: LOW directly (written for evaluators, assumes counterfactuals). Designers: MEDIUM (principles 1, 2, 3, 5, 6 transferable as rubric criteria; 4 should be flagged as optional/advanced).

### Gaps
- No 3ie guidance written specifically for small nonprofits was found.

## Q7. Theory of Change quality reviews (Vogel/DFID 2012; Hivos 2015; Comic Relief/James 2011; Rogers/UNICEF 2014; Valters)

### Takeaway
These reviews converge on the same quality criteria: usefulness, clarity, ownership; assumptions behind the arrows made explicit, sorted and evidenced; context grounding; uncertainties/unintended effects; progress markers; regular revision. Vogel's review provides ready-made criteria and practical prompts for assumptions that we can turn directly into the step-3 rubric.

### Cited Findings
- Vogel, I. (2012) "Review of the use of 'Theory of Change' in international development", DFID; based on 40 interviews with staff from 25 organisations — [UK Gov PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/57a08a5ded915d3cfd00071a/DFID_ToC_Review_VogelV7.pdf); [gov.uk R4D page](https://www.gov.uk/research-for-development-outputs/review-of-the-use-of-theory-of-change-in-international-development-review-report); [GSDRC](https://gsdrc.org/document-library/review-of-the-use-of-theory-of-change-in-international-development/)
- Vogel: "The central idea in theory of change thinking is making assumptions explicit"; "Getting depth and critical thinking on assumptions is widely agreed to be the crux of a theory of change process" — [Vogel 2012, pp. ~5, 22](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/57a08a5ded915d3cfd00071a/DFID_ToC_Review_VogelV7.pdf)
- Vogel's practical suggestions on assumptions: treat them as "things that we believe to be true", phrase as affirmative statements or "If we undertake x action, then y change will result because…"; test them from stakeholders' perspectives; brainstorm 5–8; sort into categories: causality (hypotheses about how change happens), programme implementation conditions, and context/external factors; link each assumption to a specific part of the change process; "unpick the arrow" between one change and the next; look at the programme from the beneficiaries' perspective ("How would they know about your programme? … Why should they participate?") — [Vogel 2012, pp. 30–31](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/57a08a5ded915d3cfd00071a/DFID_ToC_Review_VogelV7.pdf)
- Vogel's quality criteria: three principal criteria "usefulness, clarity and ownership"; process criteria: participatory group process, clear grounding in context (incl. political economy), sufficient time; other criteria: realistic, specific outcomes with understood "sphere of influence" and plausible road map; "Assumptions and hypotheses that lie behind the 'arrows' have been explored and captured"; evidence used to triangulate; "Uncertainties, risks and knock-on effects are captured: unintended effects, negative as well as positive"; representation "stands up to scrutiny … links and arrows are labelled with key assumptions"; progress markers identified; power analysis; ongoing review and revision — [Vogel 2012, pp. 33–35](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/57a08a5ded915d3cfd00071a/DFID_ToC_Review_VogelV7.pdf)
- Vogel on proportionality: "Unless people are able to take time to step away from the day-to-day work, then a good process will not be achieved and it may be better to work with the tools that are already in current use"; organisations simplified by "using familiar terminology, working through existing planning processes and at the level of asking key questions" — [Vogel 2012, pp. 34–35](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/57a08a5ded915d3cfd00071a/DFID_ToC_Review_VogelV7.pdf)
- Vogel on evidence: role of evidence is "to check and challenge assumptions"; triangulate between academic and community, qualitative and quantitative evidence — [Vogel 2012, section 7, p. ~40](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/57a08a5ded915d3cfd00071a/DFID_ToC_Review_VogelV7.pdf)
- Hivos: van Es, M., Guijt, I. & Vogel, I. (2015) "Theory of Change Thinking in Practice: A Stepwise Approach", CC BY-NC-SA 3.0; eight steps: clarify purpose, describe desired change, analyse current situation, identify domains of change, identify strategic priorities, map pathways of change, define monitoring/evaluation priorities, use and adaptation — [Hivos PDF](https://hivos.org/assets/2020/10/hivos_toc_guidelines.pdf); [MandE NEWS](https://mande.co.uk/2015/uncategorized/hivos-toc-guidelines-theory-of-change-thinking-in-practice-a-stepwise-approach/)
- Comic Relief: James, C. (2011) "Theory of Change Review – A report commissioned by Comic Relief"; literature + 32 interviews — [PDF via ActKnowledge](https://www.actknowledge.org/resources/documents/James_ToC.pdf); [MandE NEWS](https://mande.co.uk/?p=3797). NOTE: the Comic Relief review is by Cathy James, not by Patricia Rogers or Craig Valters (correction to the brief).
- Rogers, P. (2014) "Theory of Change", UNICEF Methodological Brief No. 2 (Impact Evaluation series): ToC explains how activities produce results contributing to intended impacts; useful to identify data to collect and how to analyse it, and as a reporting framework — [PDF mirror](https://cnxus.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Theory_of_Change_ENG.pdf); [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ucf/metbri/innpub747.html)
- Background knowledge (not re-verified): Valters, C. (2014/2015) "Theories of Change in International Development: Communication, Learning, or Accountability?" (JSRP/Asia Foundation; ODI 2015 "Theories of change: time for a radical approach to learning in development") argues ToC should be used primarily for learning, not accountability, and warns against ToC as a donor box-ticking exercise.

### Inferences
- Steps informed: 2 (Hivos "analyse current situation"), 3 (core), 4 (Vogel "progress markers"; Hivos "define M&E priorities"), 6.
- Beyond catalogue: Aspen already has 3 assumption types and plausible/feasible/testable. New elements: (a) Vogel's assumption-type split causality / implementation conditions / context — overlaps Aspen but adds "implementation conditions" as a distinct category; (b) "label the arrows" with assumptions; (c) unintended negative effects; (d) beneficiary-perspective test ("why should they participate?"); (e) "good enough" quality relative to purpose — legitimises a light ToC; (f) Hivos's "domains of change" and "use and adaptation" (revision cycle).
- Directly reusable for our step-3 rubric: usefulness, clarity (someone else can follow the logic), ownership (who was involved), assumptions per arrow, evidence per assumption, unintended effects, revision date.
- Verdict user: Hivos guide MEDIUM (readable but workshop-oriented, ~90 pp); Vogel LOW to read in full. Designers: HIGH for Vogel pp. 26–36 and Hivos steps.

### Gaps
- Did not open Hivos or James PDFs in this session; their specific quality checklists are not quoted here. The Hivos "How can I recognise good quality ToC" portal page cited by Vogel is likely offline.

## Q8. Evidence standards as a SIMPLE scale for assumptions (Nesta, Bond, Project Oracle) vs. our "own experience / data / research"

### Takeaway
All three are 5-level or 5-principle frameworks for rating evidence of a whole intervention, not single assumptions; Nesta Level 1 = "convincing theory of change drawing on existing data and research" — i.e. exactly where our users will sit. Our three-label scale (own experience / own data / external research) is a reasonable, simpler, assumption-level adaptation; Bond's "triangulation" and "voice and inclusion" suggest two optional add-ons (more than one source? did affected people confirm it?).

### Cited Findings
- Nesta Standards of Evidence: Puttick, R. & Ludlow, J. (2013) "Standards of evidence: an approach that balances the need for evidence with innovation", Nesta — five levels — [ANDE mirror](https://andeglobal.org/?p=4561); [Nesta: Centre for Social Action evidence base](https://www.nesta.org.uk/feature/centre-social-action-our-evidence-base/nestas-standards-of-evidence/)
- Nesta Level 1 (minimum for funding): organisations can give "a logical reason, or set of reasons, for why their intervention could have an impact"; at the most basic level by "producing a convincing theory of change, and drawing upon existing data and resources from other sources". Level 2: data shows some change among those receiving the intervention but does not evidence causality. Level 3: can demonstrate causation by showing less impact among those who don't receive it (comparison group) — [Nesta Centre for Social Action page](https://www.nesta.org.uk/feature/centre-social-action-our-evidence-base/nestas-standards-of-evidence/)
- Nesta also mapped standards of evidence used in UK social policy — [Nesta report](https://www.nesta.org.uk/report/mapping-standards-evidence-used-uk-social-policy/); IRIS+ references Nesta — [IRIS+ and NESTA](https://irisdev.thegiin.org/document/iris-and-nesta)
- Background knowledge (not re-verified): Level 4 = independent replication evaluation(s) confirming the conclusions; Level 5 = manuals/systems/procedures ensuring consistent replication and positive impact at scale. Original 2013 PDF (Alliance for Useful Evidence site) — URL not re-verified.
- Bond Evidence Principles (2018, UK NGO network): a tool "for assessing and improving the quality of evidence in evaluation reports, research reports and case studies", designed for NGOs; usable to review evidence, design ToRs, design MEL plans, peer review — [Bond Evidence Principles](https://www.bond.org.uk/resources/evidence-principles); [ALNAP record](https://alnap.org/help-library/resources/principles-and-checklist-for-assessing-the-quality-of-evidence/)
- Five principles: Voice and Inclusion, Appropriateness, Triangulation, Contribution, Transparency; 4 indicators per principle (20 total), each scored on a 4-point scale — [Darwin Initiative training slides citing Bond](https://www.darwininitiative.org.uk/media/ivrbrcya/9-evidence.pdf); principles confirmed in search summary of [Bond page](https://www.bond.org.uk/resources/evidence-principles)
- Project Oracle (London youth evidence hub): five-level Standards of Evidence, developed by the Social Research Unit at Dartington, loosely based on the Maryland Scientific Methods Scale; level 5 equivalent to internationally renowned programmes such as Family Nurse Partnership; statement-based rather than prescribing methods — [Nesta blog: Evaluation of Project Oracle](https://www.nesta.org.uk/blog/evaluation-project-oracle); [Institute for Employment Studies evaluation](https://www.employment-studies.co.uk/resource/resource-evaluation-project-oracle/); [Tavistock Institute "What is evidence?"](https://tavinstitute.org/projects/what-is-evidence-learning-from-the-experience-of-project-oracle-children-and-youth-evidence-hub); now under [Youth Impact](https://youthimpact.uk/project-oracle)
- Background knowledge (not re-verified): Project Oracle Level 1 = a sound ToC/model and evaluation plan; Level 2 = pre/post outcome data showing change.

### Inferences
- Steps informed: 3 (rating each assumption's evidence basis), 4–6 (evidence ambition proportionate to stage).
- Mapping to our labels: "own experience" ≈ below/at Nesta L1 (practitioner knowledge); "own data" ≈ Nesta L2 (change observed, no causality); "research" ≈ external evidence used to justify a ToC at L1, or comparison-group evidence (L3+) if it is evaluation of a similar intervention. Recommend NOT importing Nesta/Oracle levels into the user UI (they rate the whole programme and imply RCT-ladders that small projects cannot climb; risk of demotivation). Use them in designer documentation to explain why "Level 1–2 is the realistic, legitimate target".
- Bond's principles add two cheap, assumption-level quality checks our scale lacks: Triangulation ("is this supported by more than one source/perspective?") and Voice & Inclusion ("have the people affected confirmed this?"). Suggest optional modifiers on our 3-label scale rather than a new scale.
- Verdict user: LOW for the scales themselves; Bond checklist MEDIUM if simplified. Designers: HIGH (justification and calibration of our evidence labels).

### Gaps
- Exact Bond indicator wording and the 4-point scale anchors were not retrievable (Bond PDF not opened). Nesta Levels 4–5 and Project Oracle level wording not verified from primary source in this session.

## Q9. Evaluative rubrics (E. Jane Davidson et al.) — for writing our step quality rubrics

### Takeaway
Evaluative rubrics (criteria × performance levels with descriptive "evidence pictures") are the established evaluation-practice method for "how good is good enough" judgements — exactly what our per-step quality rubrics need. The free AES 2011 "Rubric Revolution" slides and Davidson's freely available JMDE articles are the canonical free entry points; her book is paywalled.

### Cited Findings
- Davidson, Wehipeihana & McKegg (2011) "The Rubric Revolution", AES conference presentation — [BetterEvaluation page](https://www.betterevaluation.org/tools-resources/rubric-revolution); [PDF](https://www.betterevaluation.org/sites/default/files/AES-2011-Rubric-Revolution-Davidson-Wehipeihana-McKegg-xx.pdf)
- Rubrics serve as interpretation guides for qualitative, quantitative and mixed evidence; as definitions of "how good is good" and "how good is good enough"; and as "evidence pictures/scenarios" to determine best fit with available evidence — [JMDE article (open access)](https://journals.sfu.ca/jmde/index.php/jmde_1/article/download/374/373/1266)
- Davidson's approach "communicates complex ideas in simple language" and integrates values explicitly into evaluation — [JMDE article](https://journals.sfu.ca/jmde/index.php/jmde_1/article/download/374/373/1266)
- Davidson keynote at AES 2010 — [PDF](https://aes.asn.au/images/images-old/stories/files/conferences/2010/Keynotes/Keynote%20Davidson.pdf)
- Davidson, E.J. (2005) "Evaluation Methodology Basics: The Nuts and Bolts of Sound Evaluation", Sage — PAYWALLED book — [Sage](https://www.sagepub.com/en-us/cam/evaluation-methodology-basics/book226129)
- "Genuine Evaluation" elements (Davidson & Rogers blog): right questions, value-based, credibly evidenced, well communicated, usable/actionable, sincere intent to use, humility, value for money — summarised in search results ([JMDE](https://journals.sfu.ca/jmde/index.php/jmde_1/article/download/374/373/1266)); blog URL not verified.
- Background knowledge (not re-verified): typical rubric levels are Excellent / Very good / Good / Adequate / Poor (sometimes "Emerging/Developing/Consolidating/Highly developed"), each with a descriptive narrative; rubrics are best co-developed with stakeholders; King, McKegg, Oakden & Wehipeihana (2013) "Rubrics: A method for surfacing values and improving the credibility of evaluation", JMDE 9(21) is open access; Oakden (2013) "Evaluation rubrics: how to ensure transparent and clear assessment that respects diverse lines of evidence" (BetterEvaluation, free).

### Inferences
- Steps informed: all (our rubric design), and step 6/later (user could judge "how good is good enough" for their own outcomes).
- Beyond catalogue: none of the baseline sources gives a method for writing quality rubrics; this is the methodological backbone for the assistant's per-step scoring. Design implications: (a) use 3–4 levels with descriptive anchors rather than numeric scores; (b) define "good enough" for the small-project context explicitly (proportionate rigour, cf. SVI); (c) separate criteria (what matters) from standards (how good on each).
- Verdict user: LOW–MEDIUM (user doesn't need to build rubrics but benefits from seeing them). Designers: VERY HIGH.

### Gaps
- Oakden 2013 and King et al. 2013 URLs not verified in this session; worth fetching for the rubric-design spec.

## Q10. Most Significant Change (brief) and other discovered methods (Ripple Effects Mapping)

### Takeaway
MSC (Davies & Dart 2005) is a free, participatory story-based method; skipping it for v1 is defensible. Ripple Effects Mapping is a free, low-tech participatory group method (one session) that could become a v2 "data collection method" option for capturing broader/unintended effects.

### Cited Findings
- MSC — background knowledge (not re-verified in this session): Davies, R. & Dart, J. (2005) "The 'Most Significant Change' (MSC) Technique: A Guide to Its Use", free PDF on mande.co.uk; collects change stories, systematically selected by stakeholder panels.
- Ripple Effects Mapping: participatory method combining Appreciative Inquiry, mind mapping, facilitated discussion and qualitative analysis to capture impact of complex programmes — "A Field Guide to Ripple Effects Mapping" (Minnesota Evaluation Studies Institute), CC BY-NC 4.0, free — [UMN Publishing](https://publishing.lib.umn.edu/?p=5198)
- UF/IFAS (2025) "Ripple Effects Mapping for Extension Program Evaluation": four key components, steps, worked example — [EDIS article](https://journals.flvc.org/edis/article/view/136938); [PDF](https://edis.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/WC482/pdf)
- University of Minnesota Extension REM page — [UMN Extension](https://extension.umn.edu/community/community-development/economy-and-development/ripple-effect-mapping)

### Inferences
- Steps informed: 5 (method option), 3 (feeding unintended effects back into ToC).
- Beyond catalogue: participatory, retrospective capture of ripple/unintended effects in one 1–2h session — feasible for small projects with a facilitator; PHINEO's method overview does not include it.
- Verdict user: MEDIUM (feasible once per year); designers: LOW–MEDIUM for v1 (method list entry only).

### Gaps
- Did not verify MSC guide URL in this session.

## Q11. Cross-cutting synthesis: what these add beyond the baseline catalogue, per step

### Takeaway
The academic hubs add four things our baseline lacks: (1) rival explanations / contribution logic (CA), (2) mechanisms and "for whom" (realist), (3) systematic capture of unintended and negative effects (Vogel, BetterEvaluation, Outcome Harvesting, REM), and (4) a method for writing evaluative rubrics (Davidson). Everything else is largely covered by PHINEO/Aspen/SVI.

### Cited Findings
- Unintended results as an explicit DEFINE task — [BetterEvaluation](https://betterevaluation.org/node/2418); as ToC quality criterion — [Vogel 2012 p. 35](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/57a08a5ded915d3cfd00071a/DFID_ToC_Review_VogelV7.pdf)
- Rival explanations as a step in CA — [onthinktanks](https://onthinktanks.org/articles/contribution-analysis-by-john-mayne/)
- "For whom, in what context" — [RAMESES II protocol](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/5/8/e008567); heterogeneity principle — [White 2009 via GSDRC](https://gsdrc.org/document-library/theory-based-impact-evaluation-principles-and-practice/)
- "Good enough" relative to purpose — [Vogel 2012 p. 33](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/57a08a5ded915d3cfd00071a/DFID_ToC_Review_VogelV7.pdf)
- Rubrics — [Rubric Revolution](https://www.betterevaluation.org/sites/default/files/AES-2011-Rubric-Revolution-Davidson-Wehipeihana-McKegg-xx.pdf)

### Inferences (step mapping; designer-facing)
| Step | New elements from academic sources | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 0 Context | Purpose & intended users of the measurement; "good enough" for purpose | BetterEvaluation FRAME; Vogel |
| 1 Target groups | "For whom (not)?" subgroups; boundary partners (direct vs. indirect) | Realist; Outcome Mapping |
| 2 Problem | (largely covered by PHINEO problem tree); Hivos "analyse current situation" incl. actors/power | Hivos; Vogel |
| 3 ToC | Label arrows with assumptions; "because…" mechanism; 3 assumption categories (causal / implementation / context); rival explanations; unintended (negative) effects; beneficiary perspective test; sphere of influence; revision date | Vogel; Realist; CA; OM |
| 4 Indicators | Indicators along the whole chain incl. reach/participation ("factual analysis"); graded progress markers; qualitative outcome descriptions | White; OM; OH |
| 5 Methods | Outcome log/harvest; REM; (MSC v2) | OH; REM |
| 6 Plan | Prioritise data collection where assumptions are weakest / contribution story is thinnest; evidence-level target realistically Nesta L1–2 | CA; Nesta |
| Rubrics | Criteria × descriptive levels; "how good is good enough" | Davidson |

- Overall user verdicts (1 h/week): use light borrowings only — CA questions, Vogel's assumption prompts, OM progress markers, outcome log. NOT recommended as full methods for the user: Realist evaluation, full Outcome Mapping, full Outcome Harvesting, 3ie counterfactual designs, Nesta/Oracle level climbing, ROMA.
