# Think tanks beat flagship universities on methods

The best free methods for Wirkungshelferin mostly do not come from Stanford, Oxford or Cambridge. They come from the evaluation-methods community (Vogel/DFID, John Mayne's Contribution Analysis, E. Jane Davidson's evaluative rubrics), from groups linked to universities (Yale/UW/IPA "right-fit evidence", Oxford's Government Outcomes Lab) and from UK think tanks (NPC, Nesta). Stanford contributes mainly through its magazine SSIR, not through its own method guide. Oxford's one practical, free tool is the GO Lab outcomes guide, and Cambridge's output targets investors. I recommend **seven additions to the catalogue**:

1. NPC's Five Types of Data
2. IPA's Goldilocks Theory of Change module together with Gugerty & Karlan's CART principles
3. Vogel's 2012 Theory of Change review
4. Mayne's Contribution Analysis
5. NPC's Theory of Change in Ten Steps
6. Davidson's evaluative rubrics
7. The IMV-Lab Praxishandbuch (Hamburg/LMU), the one German-language entry

Nesta's Standards of Evidence serve as a calibration reference for us as designers rather than as an entry for users. These sources give our baseline (PHINEO, Aspen, IRIS+/IMP, SRS, SVI, Matatihi) four things it lacks:

- explicit permission and rules for **what not to measure**
- **rival explanations and change mechanisms** at step 3
- a systematic prompt for **unintended and negative effects**
- a sound **method for writing our own quality rubrics**

The academic sources also back the PO's two decisions. A simple evidence label per assumption (own experience / data / research) fits how Nesta, ActKnowledge and IPA treat evidence. Skipping Most Significant Change in v1 is defensible, provided step 3 asks about unintended effects.

## The flagship universities supply research, not usable guides

The starting assumption, that the leading universities are where to find method guides, mostly turned out wrong.

**Stanford.** Neither Stanford PACS nor the Center for Social Innovation has a free method guide of its own. Its influence runs through SSIR. SSIR published Gugerty (University of Washington) and Karlan (Yale/IPA) on "Ten Reasons Not to Measure Impact", which introduced the **CART principles** (Credible, Actionable, Responsible, Transportable) ([SSIR](https://ssir.org/articles/entry/ten_reasons_not_to_measure_impact_and_what_to_do_instead)). It also published Ruff & Olsen's argument for context-specific, consistently documented measures over standard indicator sets ([SSIR](https://ssir.org/articles/entry/next_frontier_in_social_impact_measurement)). The book behind CART, *The Goldilocks Challenge*, is published by Oxford University Press and appears to be paywalled ([OUP](https://academic.oup.com/book/34819)). Its free practical form is IPA's Goldilocks Toolkit, built with UC Berkeley's CEGA ([FSN Network](https://fsnnetwork.org/resource/goldilocks-toolkit)).

**Oxford.** Oxford has three relevant units:

- **Saïd Business School / Skoll Centre** (Alex Nicholls). Research plus a paid executive programme; no free practitioner guide was found ([Skoll Centre](https://skollcentre.org/research)).
- **Government Outcomes Lab** (Blavatnik School). Publishes "Setting and measuring outcomes", a Creative Commons web guide. It adds **proxy and lead/progression outcomes** and warns about **perverse incentives** ("cherry picking", "creaming/parking") and outcomes "unduly influenced by factors outside the control of the provider" ([GO Lab](https://golab.bsg.ox.ac.uk/toolkit/technical-guidance/setting-measuring-outcomes/)).
- **RAMESES** (Primary Care Health Sciences). Hosts the realist-evaluation standards ([Oxford RAMESES](https://www.phc.ox.ac.uk/research/interdisciplinary-research-in-health-sciences/rameses)). These are expert methods, but their core question, "what works, for whom, in what circumstances, and why", is a useful prompt for steps 1 and 3 ([BMJ Open](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/5/8/e008567)).

**Cambridge.** CISL's "In search of impact" is an SDG portfolio dashboard for investors ([CISL](https://www.cisl.cam.ac.uk/resources/sustainable-finance-publications/in-search-of-impact-measuring-the-full-value-of-capital)). The Judge Centre for Social Innovation has no practical guide ([CCSI](https://www.jbs.cam.ac.uk/faculty-research/centres/social-innovation/)). The same holds for LSE Marshall ([LSE](https://www.lse.ac.uk/marshall-institute/about-us)).

**Other US centres.** HBS Impact-Weighted Accounts monetises corporate impacts using **593 valuation coefficients** and was spun out to IFVI in 2022 ([Embedding Project](https://embeddingproject.org/resources/impact-weighted-accounts-project)), so it is irrelevant to a small nonprofit. J-PAL (MIT) is built around randomised trials ([J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/teaching-resources-randomized-evaluations)).

The one US academic idea that matters strategically is Ebrahim & Rangan's (HBS, now Tufts) contingency framework. It says it is **"neither feasible nor desirable"** for every organisation to measure long-term impact, and that many should stop at outputs and short-term outcomes ([MandE summary](https://mande.co.uk/2010/uncategorized/the-limits-of-nonprofit-impact-a-contingency-framework-for-measuring-social-performance/)). That is exactly the permission our ~1 hour/week user needs at step 0.

**German-speaking centres.** These largely restate what PHINEO already gives us (IOOI, Wirkungstreppe, SMART):

- **CSI Heidelberg** and **WU Wien** focus on SROI, and their key guides are paid books ([Palgrave](https://palgrave.com/de/book/9783319714004)).
- **CEval** produces academic working papers ([SULB](https://jahrbib.sulb.uni-saarland.de/bitstream/20.500.11880/25497/1/01_Selbstdarstellung_CEval.pdf)).
- The exception is the **IMV-Lab Praxishandbuch** (Universität Hamburg + LMU, April 2024, BMBF-funded). Its brief URL now returns 404. A working mirror is on EPALE ([PDF](https://epale.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2024-04/Praxishandbuch_IMV-Lab.pdf)).

**Rank 1: NPC Five Types of Data** solves a problem none of our baseline sources solves well: which data a tiny project should collect first. IMP's data categories and PHINEO's method overview list options. NPC gives an order, and states that **"for most organisations, strong user, engagement, and feedback data provides a solid foundation for learning and improvement"**. Impact data should be collected only when "realistic and proportionate" ([NPC](https://www.thinknpc.org/resource-hub/five-types-of-data-framework/)). That maps onto steps 4–6 and fits the ~1 hour/week budget. Its companion, NPC's 41-page "Understanding Impact" (2020), turns a ToC into a measurement framework and includes mini-guides on sampling and research ethics ([NPC](https://www.thinknpc.org/resource-hub/understanding-impact/)). It is a good reference behind the five types, but too long for the user. It replaces NPC's older "four pillar" guide.

**Rank 2: IPA Goldilocks ToC and CART** are the strongest academic basis for "right-sized" measurement. The Goldilocks ToC PDF defines assumptions as "the conditions that have to hold for a certain part of a program to work as expected". It places them on the arrows and separates them from risks ("factors outside of the organization's control"). It names links with many assumptions as **"candidates for focused data collection"**, and warns against collecting data "just in case" ([IPA PDF](https://poverty-action.org/sites/default/files/publications/Goldilocks-Toolkit-Theory-of-Change_1.pdf)). CART's "Actionable" ("collect data you can commit to use") and "Responsible" (benefits outweigh costs, including the burden on respondents) work as a two-question filter at the end of step 4 or step 6 ([SSIR](https://ssir.org/articles/entry/ten_reasons_not_to_measure_impact_and_what_to_do_instead)). The "Ten Reasons" article also justifies a calm message at step 0: if the programme is small, new or already backed by evidence, monitoring is enough.

**Rank 3: Vogel's DFID review** is the richest free source for a step-3 rubric. It is based on **40 interviews with staff from 25 organisations**. Its central claim is that "getting depth and critical thinking on assumptions is widely agreed to be the crux of a theory of change process". It gives practical assumption prompts: phrase them as "If we do x, then y will result because…"; brainstorm 5–8; and sort them into causality, implementation conditions and context. It also gives quality criteria: usefulness, clarity and ownership; arrows labelled with assumptions; "unintended effects, negative as well as positive"; and ongoing revision ([Vogel 2012](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/57a08a5ded915d3cfd00071a/DFID_ToC_Review_VogelV7.pdf)). Two further points matter:

- Vogel says a light process using "familiar terminology" and "key questions" can be the right choice. That is a direct academic basis for our proportionate approach.
- The related Hivos stepwise guide (van Es, Guijt & Vogel 2015) is **CC BY-NC-SA 3.0**, which makes it the most reuse-friendly ToC guide found ([Hivos PDF](https://hivos.org/assets/2020/10/hivos_toc_guidelines.pdf)). At about 90 pages and workshop-oriented, it is reference material for us, not a separate entry.

**Rank 4: Contribution Analysis** covers the most important conceptual gap. PHINEO and Aspen name assumptions but never ask what else could have caused the change. SROI handles that with numbers (deadweight, attribution). CA handles it as a story a non-expert can tell. The steps, as summarised by onthinktanks, are:

1. Set out the cause–effect question.
2. Develop the ToC and the risks to it, including rival explanations.
3. Gather existing evidence.
4. Assemble the contribution story.
5. Seek more evidence.
6. Strengthen the contribution story.

([onthinktanks](https://onthinktanks.org/articles/contribution-analysis-by-john-mayne/); [BetterEvaluation](https://betterevaluation.org/node/2708)). For the user, this becomes two or three guided questions at step 3 and one prioritisation rule at step 6. Mayne's open-access 2019 update is the designer reference ([CJPE](https://ces.journals.uvic.ca/index.php/cjpe/article/view/284)).

**Rank 5: NPC's Ten Steps** matches our flow so closely (situation analysis, target groups, impact, outcomes, activities, mechanisms, …, assumptions) that it validates our order. It also adds one cheap element our baseline lacks: an explicit **"change mechanism"** per link, a "because…" that explains why an activity should cause a change ([NPC](https://www.thinknpc.org/resource-hub/creating-your-theory-of-change-npcs-practical-guide/)). It is the plain-language counterpart to the realist "mechanism" concept, without the realist apparatus.

**Rank 6: Davidson's evaluative rubrics** are not for users, but none of our baseline sources says how to write a quality rubric. Davidson's approach supplies that. Rubrics define "how good is good" and "how good is good enough". They describe each level with "evidence pictures" against which a best fit is judged, and they work for qualitative, quantitative and mixed evidence ([JMDE](https://journals.sfu.ca/jmde/index.php/jmde_1/article/download/374/373/1266); [Rubric Revolution](https://www.betterevaluation.org/tools-resources/rubric-revolution)). This should become the template for every per-step rubric in the assistant.

**Rank 7: IMV-Lab Praxishandbuch** is the one German academic source worth an entry. Its Anhang I yes/no checklist, with questions such as "Wurde der Umfang und die Grenzen der zu bewertenden Wirkungen festgelegt?", is a ready-made German seed for rubrics at steps 0 and 3–4. Its survey and interview chapter covers step 5 better than PHINEO's overview ([PDF](https://epale.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2024-04/Praxishandbuch_IMV-Lab.pdf)). At about 94 printed pages, point users to the checklist and the questionnaire chapter only.

**Nesta Standards of Evidence** stays as designer calibration rather than a user entry. Its five levels rate a whole intervention:

- **L1**: "describe what you do and why it matters, logically, coherently and convincingly"
- **L2**: "data that shows positive change, but you cannot confirm you caused this"
- **L3**: causality shown with a comparison group
- **L4–L5**: replication and manuals

([Nesta PDF](https://media.nesta.org.uk/documents/standards_of_evidence.pdf)). L1–L2 is the realistic, legitimate target for our users, and our copy should say so. Showing the full ladder in the UI risks demotivating them.

## Designers and end users need different slices of the same sources

Most of the value of these sources lies in the **prompts, checks and templates we build into the assistant**, not in documents the user reads. The user's ~1 hour/week rules out assigned reading. Even the shortest recommended PDFs should appear as optional "learn more" links.

**For us as designers of the method guide and rubrics:**

- **Davidson's rubrics** give the structure for every rubric: 3–4 levels with descriptive anchors instead of numeric scores, criteria kept separate from standards, and "good enough" defined explicitly for the small-project context.
- **Vogel** (pp. 26–36) supplies the step-3 criteria, and **Mayne** supplies the logic behind the contribution claim.
- **Nesta** supplies the calibration ("L1–L2 is success").
- **BetterEvaluation's Rainbow Framework** is a completeness checklist. Its DEFINE cluster ("develop initial description", "develop programme theory", "identify possible unintended results") maps almost 1:1 onto steps 0–3, and DESCRIBE maps onto steps 4–6 ([BetterEvaluation](https://betterevaluation.org/node/2418)). It stays free and open access under the Global Evaluation Initiative ([BetterEvaluation news](https://betterevaluation.org/node/2940)). It is too large for a user to navigate.
- The **DeGEval Glossar (2017)** ([PDF](https://www.degeval.org/fileadmin/content/Z03_Publikationen/DeGEval-Standards/2017_11_21_DeGEval-StandardsGlossar_ES_1_.pdf)) and **Univation's Eval-Wiki** ([Eval-Wiki](https://eval-wiki.org/glossar/Indikator)) are authoritative German references to check our plain-language glossary against.
- DeGEval's 2004 self-evaluation recommendations target our user type: practitioners who evaluate their own work with "kaum oder wenig" methods training ([PDF](https://www.degeval.org/fileadmin/content/Z03_Publikationen/DeGEval_-_Empfehlungen_Selbstevaluation.pdf)). Its four standard groups (Nützlichkeit, Durchführbarkeit, Fairness, Genauigkeit) could become a four-question check at steps 5–6. That use is my inference, not something DeGEval prescribes.

**For the end user**, the same sources become short in-flow moments:

| Step | Built-in element | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 0 Context | "Who will use these results, and for what?" plus "Which result level can you realistically claim: outputs, short-term outcomes, or more?" | BetterEvaluation FRAME; Ebrahim & Rangan; Gugerty & Karlan |
| 1 Target groups | "For whom might this work less well, or not at all?"; direct vs indirect target groups | Realist / RAMESES; Outcome Mapping |
| 3 ToC | A "because…" field per arrow; one or two "what else could explain this change?"; one "what could go wrong or happen unintentionally?"; closing check "plausible, doable, testable?" | NPC; Mayne; Vogel; ActKnowledge |
| 4 Indicators | Indicators along the whole chain, including reach and participation; "how many of whom will reach what level by when?"; a warning against indicators that reward working only with easy participants | White/3ie; ActKnowledge; GO Lab |
| 5 Methods | The Five Types of Data order; questionnaire basics | NPC; IMV-Lab |
| 6 Plan | Collect first where critical assumptions are least supported; two-question CART filter ("Will we use it? Is it worth participants' time?"); a baseline value | IPA Goldilocks; CART; ZEWO |

Several smaller sources supply one element each without needing an entry:

- **ActKnowledge's "ToC Basics"** gives the plausible / doable / testable check and the four-part indicator formula. It overlaps heavily with Aspen, from the same tradition ([PDF](https://www.theoryofchange.org/wp-content/uploads/toco_library/pdf/ToCBasics.pdf)).
- **Howard White's six principles** of theory-based impact evaluation include **"rigorous factual analysis"**: check that each link actually happened (reach, participation, quality). That makes the lower levels of the Wirkungstreppe legitimate evidence ([GSDRC](https://gsdrc.org/document-library/theory-based-impact-evaluation-principles-and-practice/)).
- **Outcome Mapping** offers graded progress markers ("expect / like / love to see") as a gentler alternative to SMART targets for behaviour change ([IDRC PDF](https://www.theoryofchange.org/wp-content/uploads/toco_library/pdf/2001-Earl-Book-Outcome-mapping-building-learning.pdf)). The labels come from background knowledge and need checking against the original.
- **ZEWO's free Impact Tool Light** models step 6 as a "Fortschritts-Tabelle" (which data, when, against which comparison) with a mandatory baseline ([Zewo](https://zewo.ch/de/zewo-impact-tool-wirkung-zeigen/)). It is also a direct competitor reference: a guided, German, six-step tool with an optional AI SMART-check.

## The evidence label and the MSC skip both hold, with small refinements

**The evidence-basis label.** No source offers a ready-made evidence scale at the level of single assumptions. Nesta, Project Oracle and Bond all rate whole interventions or reports. So the PO's three-label scale (own experience / data / research) fills a real gap rather than duplicating a standard. Three sources support it:

- Nesta's L1 explicitly expects organisations to "draw upon existing data and research from other sources" ([Nesta PDF](https://media.nesta.org.uk/documents/standards_of_evidence.pdf)). That matches the "research" label.
- ActKnowledge says rationales and assumptions are "often … supported by research, strengthening the plausibility of the theory" ([ToC Basics](https://www.theoryofchange.org/wp-content/uploads/toco_library/pdf/ToCBasics.pdf)).
- Vogel says evidence exists "to check and challenge assumptions" ([Vogel 2012](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/57a08a5ded915d3cfd00071a/DFID_ToC_Review_VogelV7.pdf)).

The sources suggest four small refinements. The first two are my synthesis, not any single source's scale:

1. **Add a fourth state, "not yet supported / unsure".** Users will otherwise force a label onto a hunch.
2. **Link that state to step 6** with one rule: an unsure, critical assumption goes into the data collection plan. This is the IPA Goldilocks prioritisation logic ([IPA PDF](https://poverty-action.org/sites/default/files/publications/Goldilocks-Toolkit-Theory-of-Change_1.pdf)) and Mayne's "seek evidence where the contribution story is weakest", applied at the level of single assumptions.
3. **Make the "data" label clearly mean the project's own data**, so it stays distinct from "research by others".
4. **Optionally add two Bond Evidence Principles** as yes/no modifiers rather than a new scale ([Bond](https://www.bond.org.uk/resources/evidence-principles)):
   - **Triangulation**: is the assumption backed by more than one source?
   - **Voice and Inclusion**: have the affected people confirmed it?

Two related distinctions help the guide's wording without adding UI. IPA separates assumptions (inside the causal chain) from risks (outside the organisation's control). ActKnowledge separates assumptions (conditions that already hold) from preconditions (outcomes the project must bring about).

**Skipping Most Significant Change.** The research notes judge the skip defensible but contain no verified source on MSC itself. The Davies & Dart (2005) guide is free, but its URL was not checked. The real cost of skipping MSC is losing a channel for unexpected change. Three cheaper elements recover most of that:

- Vogel's criterion on unintended negative and positive effects at step 3
- BetterEvaluation's "identify possible unintended results" task
- an optional "outcome log" at step 5, borrowed from Outcome Harvesting: who changed, what changed, how did we contribute, who can confirm ([Wilson-Grau & Britt PDF](https://www.usaidlearninglab.org/system/files/resource/files/Outome%20Harvesting%20Brief%20FINAL%202012-05-2-1_0.pdf))

For v2, **Ripple Effects Mapping** is a better candidate than MSC. It is a single participatory group session for capturing wider effects, with a free field guide under **CC BY-NC 4.0** ([UMN Publishing](https://publishing.lib.umn.edu/?p=5198)).

## Conclusion

The search for university method guides mainly shows that academic credibility and practical usability sit in different places. The flagship universities lend legitimacy through their people and magazines: Karlan and Gugerty via SSIR, Ebrahim via HBS, Oxford's GO Lab and RAMESES. The usable tools come from think tanks and the evaluation profession. For Wirkungshelferin this is useful, because the most valuable academic ideas are about restraint rather than rigour: stop at the result level you can credibly claim, collect only data you will use, check the weakest assumptions first, and tell a contribution story rather than prove attribution. Those ideas, together with Davidson's rubric method, can give the assistant a coherent stance on proportionality that the existing German baseline (PHINEO, SRS) only implies.
